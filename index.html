Chapter 1

The Light From Yesterday

When we look at the sky, it feels like we are seeing the universe in the present. But the first lesson is simple and strange: we never see distant objects exactly as they are right now.

Light takes time to travel. When a photon leaves a distant star, it carries information about that star at the moment the light began its journey. By the time the photon reaches Earth, that information is already from the past.

Think of it like a video message with a delay. If the Sun could send Earth a picture, the picture would take about 8 minutes to arrive. We would not receive the Sun as it is this instant. We would receive a message from the Sun's recent past.

Chapter 2

The Clock That Fell Behind

Time is not one universal metronome. In special relativity, two observers can reunite and honestly disagree about how much time passed.

At highway speeds? You would never notice it. But push a rocket toward the speed of light, and things get weird fast: Earth can age by years while the traveler ages by much less.

In this simulator, velocity means the spacecraft's speed relative to Earth. At 0%, the ship is sharing Earth's inertial frame, so the two clocks match in this simplified comparison.

Chrona observes

A clock does not only belong to a place. It belongs to a path through spacetime. Change the path enough, and two clocks that once agreed will return with different histories.

Key idea Near light speed, motion through space changes motion through time.

The Lorentz factor compares coordinate time on Earth with proper time on the traveler's clock. In this simulator, Earth is the reference frame, so \(\Delta \tau = \Delta t / \gamma\).

When \(v = 0\), \(\gamma = 1\), and both clocks agree. As \(v\) approaches \(c\), the denominator \(\sqrt{1 - (v/c)^2}\) approaches zero, so \(\gamma\) grows without bound. That is why the slider gets dramatic near the end.

Real-world check

Einstein's special relativity is measurable. In the Hafele-Keating experiment, atomic clocks flown on aircraft shifted slightly compared with clocks left on Earth. GPS systems also account for relativistic clock differences every day.

The Earth clock is treated as the reference clock. The moving spacecraft follows a different direction through spacetime, so its experienced time is smaller by a factor of gamma. Imagine walking at a fixed speed: if you walk straight north, all your progress is northward. If you veer east, some of that same fixed motion points east, so your northward progress slows. In the same spirit, motion through space reduces the time accumulated along the traveler's path compared with Earth.

This simplified model assumes constant velocity, no acceleration period, no gravity difference, and an ideal inertial comparison. It also stops below light speed because objects with mass cannot reach c.

Chapter 3

Where Time Sinks

Gravity changes time too. Not metaphorically. In general relativity, mass curves spacetime, and clocks deeper in a gravitational field tick slower than clocks far away from that mass.

This simulator uses the Schwarzschild time dilation formula for a stationary clock outside a non-rotating spherical object. A simplified model, yes. But the direction is real: stronger gravity means slower local time.

Chrona observes

Near mass, time does not break. It bends quietly. The clock still ticks, but compared with a far observer, each second carries a little less future.

Formula
\[ t_{\text{local}} = t_{\text{far}}\sqrt{1 - \frac{R_s}{r}} \]
\[ R_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2} \]
Rs means Schwarzschild radius, the event-horizon radius for a non-rotating black hole with mass M.
Technical note
Real-world check

GPS satellites must correct for both velocity time dilation and gravitational time dilation. Their clocks do not tick at exactly the same rate as clocks on Earth's surface.

Learn the Math

The deeper version comes from the Schwarzschild metric, the general-relativity solution for spacetime outside a non-rotating spherical mass.

\[ ds^2 = -\left(1-\frac{R_s}{r}\right)c^2dt^2 + \left(1-\frac{R_s}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 + r^2d\Omega^2 \]

For a stationary clock, \(dr = 0\), so the local proper time becomes \(d\tau = dt\sqrt{1 - R_s/r}\). That is the factor the simulator applies to the far-away clock.

The formula compares a clock at radius r with a clock very far from the mass. The closer r gets to the Schwarzschild radius, the smaller the square-root factor becomes, so the local clock records less time.

A better analogy is climbing out of a deep gravitational well. Light leaving the lower region loses energy as it climbs, so its wave crests arrive farther apart to a distant observer. That stretching is gravitational redshift, and it is why a clock deep in the well appears to tick slower from far away.

Conditions matter: this model assumes a spherical, non-rotating mass, a stationary clock, and a position outside the Schwarzschild radius. It does not include spin, tides, acceleration, atmospheric effects, or whether a person could survive at that location.

The Fold That Might Hold

A wormhole is the shortcut everyone wants. The catch: it only appears in some mathematical solutions of general relativity. It is not a proven machine, and no usable wormhole has been observed.

Chapter 5

The Knot in Yesterday

Traveling to the future follows from known physics. Traveling to the past is far more uncertain. In general relativity, the serious mathematical language for this is Closed Timelike Curves (CTCs): paths through spacetime that loop back and can intersect their own past.

Physicists and philosophers explore several hypotheses for the causality constraints that would follow. Each one protects cause and effect in a different way, but none has become a working time machine.

Key idea Past time travel is both a geometry problem and a logic problem. CTCs are allowed in some mathematical solutions, but no experiment has shown that a usable path into the past can exist.

Reality Check

The Walls Around Time Travel

Chrona shows where physics permits strange journeys, but it also has to show where nature pushes back. The hardest part of time travel is not imagining a path through spacetime; it is surviving the limits built into thermodynamics, relativity, and causality.

Limit 1

The Asymmetry of Time

The second law of thermodynamics gives time an arrow.

You can slow your own clock and arrive in the future, but the universe strongly resists a return to the past. In a closed system, entropy tends to increase. A glass can shatter into disorder very easily; making every fragment, air molecule, sound wave, and heat trace reverse into the original cup would require an absurdly precise global undoing.

The lesson is simple: past travel is not just a navigation problem. It asks the universe to reverse the statistical direction that ordinary time follows.

Limit 2

The Cosmic Speed Limit

No object with mass can match or exceed \(c\).

Special relativity does not let a massive spacecraft accelerate through the speed of light to make time run backward. As velocity approaches \(c\), the Lorentz factor grows without bound, so the energy and momentum required for more acceleration also grow without bound.

\[ E = \gamma mc^2 \]

The closer the ship gets to light speed, the more the universe demands. Reaching the threshold would require infinite energy, so velocity alone cannot cross into negative time.

Limit 3

Cosmic Censorship and Chronology Protection

The universe may prevent usable loops into the past.

Stephen Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture suggests that the laws of physics may stop closed timelike curves before they become usable. A wormhole time loop, for example, might trigger runaway quantum energy fluctuations near the throat.

A useful analogy is microphone feedback: a tiny signal repeats, amplifies, and becomes destructive. In a speculative time machine, the feedback would be energy rather than sound, potentially destroying the shortcut before anyone could use it.

Limit 4

The Horizon Problem

A time machine cannot send you before the machine exists.

In a wormhole time-machine idea, one mouth stays relatively still while the other is moved at relativistic speed or placed in a stronger gravitational field. The two mouths build up a clock difference, turning the throat into a shortcut between different times.

But the shortcut only works after the connected throat exists. Even in this speculative setup, the earliest reachable moment is the creation or activation of the machine. That means no visiting the dinosaurs unless the machine was already waiting there.

Conclusion

What Chrona Leaves Behind

Time travel is not one idea. It is a spectrum: some parts are already built into everyday physics, and some parts remain at the edge of mathematics, engineering, and imagination. Chrona begins with the simplest version of time travel: looking across space means looking backward, because light needs time to arrive.

From there, the project moves into relativity. Speed and gravity do not make time disappear, but they do change how much time a clock experiences along its path. That is not fantasy. Atomic clocks, satellites, and precise navigation systems already have to respect these effects. Tiny at human speeds. Unavoidable at cosmic scales.

The deeper question is the past. Wormholes, closed timelike curves, paradoxes, and chronology protection show that past travel is not just difficult; it may be forbidden by the structure of the universe. Chrona does not claim that time machines are real. It shows the difference between what physics has confirmed, what equations allow us to imagine, and what nature may never permit.

Real physics

Light delay, velocity time dilation, and gravitational time dilation are measurable.

Open questions

Wormholes and CTCs are mathematical possibilities, not demonstrated technologies.

Final idea

The future is reachable by changing your path. The past remains protected by causality.

Debugging record
